Floor supports

Crawlspace
Stabilization

Floors that dip toward the middle of the house or flex when you walk across them are not a flooring problem. They are a support problem, and it is underneath, where the original piers have settled or the framing has given way.

What has happened

The supports were
never built to
last this long

A crawlspace floor system is simple: joists span between a perimeter wall and one or more central girders, and those girders are held up by piers spaced out along their length. When a pier settles or fails, the girder sags, and every joist sitting on it comes down with it. That is the dip you feel walking through the middle of the house.

In older Panhandle homes, the failures we find are remarkably consistent:

  • Dry-stacked block piers set on little or no footing, leaning or crumbling after decades in damp soil.
  • Wood posts and shims in direct contact with the ground, wicking moisture and rotting from the bottom up.
  • Undersized screw jacks installed as a temporary fix twenty years ago and never revisited.
  • Piers on inadequate footings that were fine for the original load and have been slowly pressing into soft soil ever since.
  • Rotted or termite-damaged girders that have lost the section needed to span between supports.

The fix is to put a proper support system underneath: adjustable steel posts bearing on engineered footings, at the right spacing, with the framing above them repaired where it needs to be.

Symptoms

How it shows up
on the floor above

Crawlspace support failure is gradual, so people adapt to it without noticing. If a visitor comments on your floors, take it seriously.

01

A dip toward the center

Set a ball on the floor and watch where it goes. Movement toward the middle of the house points squarely at the central girder line.

02

Bounce or spring underfoot

Floors that move when you walk past a china cabinet have lost effective support, whether from pier settlement or from joists that have lost strength.

03

Gaps at baseboard and trim

When the floor drops away from the wall, trim separates. It shows up first at interior partition walls that sit over the girder.

04

Doors that swing open on their own

An interior door that will not stay where you put it is a good indication that its frame is no longer plumb, because the floor beneath it has moved.

The repair

What we install
and how

The objective is a permanent support path from the girder to soil that can carry it, and enough adjustability to bring the floor back toward level without cracking everything upstairs.

The support system
  • Excavation to firm bearing soil at each post location
  • A compacted stone base and an engineered concrete footing
  • Galvanized adjustable steel posts, sized to the load they carry
  • Correct post spacing along the girder rather than wherever is convenient
  • Load transferred gradually, over days or weeks where the house needs it
  • Posts left adjustable, so fine corrections remain possible later
Framing repairs
  • Sistering or replacing joists that have rotted or been insect damaged
  • Girder repair or replacement where the section is compromised
  • Subfloor repair where moisture has delaminated it
  • Removing failed block piers and wood shims for good
  • Correcting the moisture source, so new lumber does not follow the old
  • Elevation readings before and after, so the change is documented
FAQs

Questions we
get asked

Can my floor be made perfectly level again?

Usually it can be improved substantially, and often a great deal. Whether it goes all the way back to level depends on how long it has been sagging and what has been finished on top of it since.

Lifting a floor too far or too fast cracks drywall, tile and plaster that have spent years adapting to the settled position. We lift gradually and stop at the point where further correction would cost you more in finish damage than it gains. We will tell you where that point is before we start.

How long does the work take?

Most crawlspace support jobs run one to three days on site. Where a floor needs to be raised gradually, we install the posts in one visit and return over the following weeks to adjust in increments. That staged approach is deliberate — it is what protects the finishes upstairs.

Do you replace rotted joists too?

Yes. Support posts do not help a joist that has lost its structural section, so framing repair is part of the same scope. We sister or replace joists, repair or replace compromised girders, and address damaged subfloor.

We also identify what caused the rot. Replacing wood without fixing the moisture that destroyed it just resets the clock.

Is this something a handyman can do with bottle jacks?

It is done that way often, and we get called out to correct the results regularly. The recurring problems are the same: no real footing beneath the jack, so it presses into the soil and the floor drops again; jacks not rated for sustained structural load; and lifting too far, too fast, which cracks finishes throughout the house.

The posts themselves are the cheap part. The footing under them, the spacing, and the rate of lift are what make it a permanent repair.
