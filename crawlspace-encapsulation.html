Crawlspace
Encapsulation

Sealing the crawlspace off from the ground and the outside air. It is the single highest-value improvement most Panhandle homes with a crawlspace can make — for the structure, for the air you breathe, and for what you pay to cool the house.

Why it matters here

Venting a crawlspace
on the Gulf Coast
makes it wetter

Foundation vents were built into millions of homes on the theory that outside air dries a crawlspace out. In a hot, humid coastal climate, the opposite happens for much of the year.

Pull 90°F air at 80% relative humidity through a vent and across surfaces sitting at 70°F — ductwork, joists, the ground itself — and that air hits its dew point. The moisture condenses out and stays. Every summer day, your vents are effectively pumping water into the space under your floor.

On top of that, bare soil in an unsealed crawlspace releases a substantial amount of water vapor continuously, and it has nowhere to go but up. Because air moves upward through a house, a large share of the air on your first floor started life in the crawlspace. Whatever is down there, you are breathing.

Encapsulation reverses all of it: seal the ground, close the vents, insulate the perimeter, and control what humidity remains with a dehumidifier sized for the space.

What an open crawlspace costs you

The damage happens
where you never look

Crawlspaces are out of sight, so the deterioration is usually well advanced by the time it announces itself upstairs.

01

Rot and fungal growth

Sustained humidity above roughly 60% lets wood-decay fungi go to work on joists, girders and subfloor. It does not need standing water — damp air is enough.

02

Pests and termites

Damp, dark and undisturbed is exactly the environment termites, roaches and rodents look for. Softened, moist wood is also far easier for termites to attack.

03

Higher power bills

Ductwork sweating in humid air loses capacity, and your system works harder to pull moisture out of air that keeps being re-supplied from below. Dry air is simply cheaper to cool.

04

Musty odors and poor air

That smell is not confined downstairs. It rises with the air, along with mold spores and anything else the space is producing.

What a proper
encapsulation involves

Encapsulation is not just rolling out plastic. Anything less than the full system leaves a path for moisture, and a partial job usually performs worse than none at all.

The work
  • Clear out debris, old insulation and failed vapor barrier
  • Correct standing water and drainage first — a liner over wet ground solves nothing
  • Treat and repair damaged framing where needed
  • Seal and insulate the rim joist and band area
  • Close and seal foundation vents and any other outside-air paths
  • Install a reinforced vapor barrier across the ground and up the walls
  • Seal every seam, penetration and pier with the correct tape and sealant
  • Install and set a dehumidifier sized for the space, with a drain line
What you get
  • Humidity held at a level where wood decay and mold cannot progress
  • A clean, dry, sealed space you can actually store things in
  • Cleaner air on the floor above, since that air comes from down here
  • Ductwork that stops sweating and losing capacity
  • A meaningfully less attractive environment for termites and pests
  • Protection for the structural repairs underneath it
  • A crawlspace an inspector can walk into at resale without wincing
FAQs

Questions we
get asked

Is encapsulation worth the cost?

It is one of the few home improvements that pays back in more than one direction. You get a measurable reduction in heating and cooling costs from conditioning drier air, you stop the ongoing decay of the framing your house sits on, and you improve the air quality on the floor above.

Compare it against what it prevents. Replacing rotted joists and subfloor, or remediating a mold problem, costs multiples of sealing the space properly in the first place.

Should I close my foundation vents?

In this climate, yes, as part of a complete encapsulation. Closing vents on their own without sealing the ground and controlling humidity can make things worse, because you trap the moisture the soil is still releasing.

Sealed and dehumidified is the goal. Vented is the problem. Half-sealed is the worst of both.

Do I need a dehumidifier if the crawlspace is sealed?

In the Panhandle, effectively always. A vapor barrier stops the ground from adding moisture, but it cannot remove what is already in the air, and no seal is perfect. A properly sized dehumidifier with a dedicated drain line is what actually holds the space at a safe humidity year round.

It is a small, unglamorous piece of equipment that determines whether the whole system works.

Will encapsulation fix my sagging floor?

No, and any contractor who tells you otherwise is selling the wrong product. Encapsulation controls moisture; it does not carry load. If your floors sag or bounce, the supports underneath need attention, and that is a separate repair.

The two do belong together, though. We generally stabilize and repair the structure first, then encapsulate, so the moisture that caused the rot is not left in place to start over on new lumber.

How long does encapsulation take?

Most crawlspaces are a one to three day job depending on square footage, how much cleanout and repair is needed first, and access. If drainage work or structural repairs come first, we sequence those ahead of the liner and give you the full schedule up front.
